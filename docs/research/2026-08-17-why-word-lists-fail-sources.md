# Why word lists fail: evidence brief

> Date: 2026-08-17
> Use: source pack for the public article `/blog/why-word-lists-fail`. Not the article itself. No product claims.
> Rule: quote what the study measured. Do not convert savings scores into “you forget 70% of real words in a day.” Do not treat flashcards as the villain. The failure mode is a generic, unreviewed, decontextualized list.

Correction up front: Roediger and Karpicke 2006 is in *Psychological Science*, not *Science*. The *Science* paper is Karpicke and Roediger 2008.

---

## Strongest 4–6 claims for a general-audience methods piece

Use these. They are independently replicated, close to the reader’s problem, and easy to state without overclaiming.

1. **Unreviewed material fades fast.** Ebbinghaus’s savings curve, replicated by Murre and Dros (2015), shows the steepest loss in the first hours and day. Say “relearning gets much harder if you never come back,” not “70% of vocabulary is gone tomorrow.”
2. **Trying to recall beats rereading.** Roediger and Karpicke (2006) and Karpicke and Roediger (2008) show that a later test is better after retrieval practice than after extra study, even when extra study feels better in the moment.
3. **Spacing beats cramming.** Cepeda et al. (2006) and Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate distributed practice as high-utility. The useful gap grows with how long you want to remember.
4. **Knowing a frequency list is not the same as being able to read.** Hu and Nation (2000), Nation (2006), and Laufer and Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) put unassisted comprehension near 95–98% coverage of running words. A list of “the most common 2,000 words” does not by itself put those words into the texts a person actually meets.
5. **Semantic sets can interfere.** Tinkham (1993, 1997) and Finkbeiner and Nicol (2003) found that new words presented as a category (fruits, animals, colours) are harder than unrelated or story-linked sets. Use this as “don’t dump a theme pack in one sitting,” and mention the later mixed results.
6. **Lists can teach form–meaning quickly; they do not automatically teach use.** Prince (1996) and Laufer (2003) are the honest comparison: translation lists often win on immediate recall; reading-only is slow; learners still need encounters in text and a chance to retrieve and use the word.

Leave out, or demote, encoding specificity as a lead claim. Godden and Baddeley (1975) is famous; later reviews show a small, brittle environmental effect. Depth of processing is real but Craik and Lockhart (1972) is a framework, not a vocabulary experiment. Interest and self-selection are plausible and weaker as a single citation.

What works instead, if the article needs a closing frame: meet the word in something you actually read or hear; retrieve it later, spaced out; keep a short context or example with it; do not study a whole semantic set at once; prefer words you have a reason to need.

## 1. Forgetting curve

**Finding.** Ebbinghaus (1885/1913) learned nonsense-syllable rows to criterion and measured *savings*: how much less time relearning took after 20 minutes, 1 hour, 9 hours, 1 day, 2 days, 6 days, and 31 days. Savings fell from about 0.58 at 20 minutes to 0.44 at 1 hour, 0.34 at 1 day, 0.25 at 6 days, and 0.21 at 31 days (figures as tabulated by Murre and Dros from the 1880/1885 reports). The drop is steepest early; later loss is slower. Murre and Dros (2015) had one Dutch-speaking subject spend about 70 hours on a close replication. Their curve matched Ebbinghaus and an earlier German replication (Heller, Mack, and Seitz) except at 31 days, where Dros saved much less. They conclude the classic curve has been replicated and is not perfectly smooth; there is probably a small upward jump from the 24-hour point.

**How to use it.** A list you never reopen behaves like a once-learned row. The article can say that unused traces fade fastest in the first hours and day, and that coming back to relearn is cheaper than starting over. Do not treat savings as free-recall percentage, and do not imply the stimuli were real L2 words.

**Strength.** Direction of the curve is robust. Magnitude popularized in blogs (“forget 70% in 24 hours”) is a loose reading of 1-day savings around 0.34. Single-subject designs, nonsense syllables, and the savings method all limit how precisely the numbers travel to vocabulary apps.

**Citations.**

- Ebbinghaus, H. (1885/1913). *Memory: A contribution to experimental psychology* (H. A. Ruger & C. E. Bussenius, Trans.). Teachers College. Chapter 7 is the time function. https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Ebbinghaus/index.htm and https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Ebbinghaus/memory7.htm

## 2. Depth of processing / elaboration

**Finding.** Craik and Lockhart (1972) argued that memory depends on how deeply an item is processed, not only on how long it sits in a short-term store. Structural or phonemic analysis is shallow; semantic analysis is deeper and more durable. Craik and Tulving (1975) then ran the standard experiment: the same words were judged for case, rhyme, or sentence fit. Semantic judgments produced much higher later recognition and recall than case or rhyme judgments, even when presentation time was controlled. The paper is about encoding operations on familiar L1 words, not about L2 word lists.

**How to use it.** Isolated list study that only pairs a form with a one-word gloss is closer to a shallow operation. Meeting the word in a sentence you understand, or generating a use, is closer to the semantic condition. Do not write “Craik proved word lists fail.”

**Strength.** The levels-of-processing idea is foundational and widely replicated for encoding quality. It is a framework, and “depth” is hard to define independently of the memory test. Transfer to L2 vocabulary is inferential. For L2-specific elaboration, prefer Joe (1998) on generation during text tasks, or Laufer and Hulstijn (2001) on involvement load (need, search, evaluation).

**Citations.**

- Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 11*(6), 671–684. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(72)80001-X
- Craik, F. I. M., & Tulving, E. (1975). Depth of processing and the retention of words in episodic memory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 104*(3), 268–294. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.104.3.268

## 3. Encoding specificity / context-dependent memory

**Finding.** Tulving and Thomson (1973) showed that a retrieval cue works when it matches what was stored at study, even if a “stronger” associate exists. Memory is not a pile of items; it is item-plus-encoding-context. Godden and Baddeley (1975) had divers learn word lists on land or underwater and test in the same or the other environment. Same-environment recall was higher (dry–dry 13.5, wet–wet 11.4, crossed conditions 8.6 and 8.4). Smith and Vela (2001) later meta-analyzed environmental context-dependent memory and found a reliable but modest average effect; mentally reinstating the study context can substitute for being in the same room, and the effect shrinks when the material is richly encoded.

**How to use it.** A word learned as an isolated pair is stored with a thin cue. A word met in a conversation or a sentence has more of the cues you will need later. Do not tell readers they must review flashcards in the café where they first saw the word. That overreads Godden and Baddeley.

**Strength.** Encoding specificity as a principle is strong. The scuba result is real and small-*n*. Everyday “study in the exam room” advice is oversold. This is the weakest of the nine claims as a reason that *generic word lists* fail.

**Citations.**

- Godden, D. R., & Baddeley, A. D. (1975). Context-dependent memory in two natural environments: On land and underwater. *British Journal of Psychology, 66*(3), 325–331. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1975.tb01468.x

## 4. Retrieval practice beats rereading

**Finding.** Roediger and Karpicke (2006, *Psychological Science*) had students study short prose passages, then either restudy or take a free-recall test without feedback. After 5 minutes, restudy looked as good or better. After 2 days or 1 week, prior testing produced substantially more recall. Repeated study also raised confidence, so the worse long-term strategy felt better. Karpicke and Roediger (2008, *Science*) used Swahili–English pairs. Students who kept retrieving the whole set recalled about 80% a week later. Students who dropped a pair from practice once they had recalled it once remembered far less, even if they had seen those pairs as often during study. Retrieval, not mere exposure, did the work.

**How to use it.** A word list you reread is the restudy condition. A card or prompt that makes you produce the word is the test condition. Immediate fluency is a bad guide.

**Strength.** Very strong. One of the most replicated effects in learning science. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate practice testing as high utility across ages and materials. Caveats: most 2006 materials were prose, not L2 lists; the 2008 *Science* paper is the closer vocabulary analogue. Feedback still helps when retrieval fails. The article should not say “never look at the answer.”

## 5. Spacing effect

**Finding.** Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, and Rohrer (2006) meta-analyzed 317 experiments and 839 assessments of distributed versus massed practice in verbal recall. Distributed practice beat massing. The optimal gap is not a fixed number of days; it scales with the retention interval. For tests a month or more later, gaps of days to weeks beat same-day cramming. Every study they found with a retention interval longer than a month showed a benefit of spreading learning across weeks or months. Dunlosky et al. (2013) independently rate distributed practice as high utility, next to practice testing. Ebbinghaus himself already noted, in the 1913 translation of Chapter 8, that 38 distributed repetitions of a 12-syllable series matched about 68 massed ones.

**How to use it.** A 500-word list done in one weekend is massed practice. Meeting 15 of those words again next week and next month is distributed practice. Random daily flashcards can implement spacing; a printed chapter list usually does not.

**Strength.** Very strong as a laboratory and educational effect. Exact “best interval” calculators in apps go beyond what Cepeda 2006 specifies. L2 vocabulary spacing has extra support from Bahrick and Phelps (1987) on Spanish over years and from later work by Nakata, but those are backups, not required for the article.

**Citations.**

## 6. Semantic clustering interference

**Finding.** Tinkham (1993) taught artificial L2 words either in semantic clusters (all fruits, all furniture) or in unrelated sets. Semantic clusters took more trials. Tinkham (1997) split “semantic” (same category) from “thematic” (frog, pond, hop, slimy). Semantic sets still hindered; thematic sets helped. Finkbeiner and Nicol (2003) taught English speakers new L2 labels in category sets or mixed sets. Category presentation slowed both encoding and later translation. They treat this as interference among new form–meaning links that share a semantic neighbour.

**Contradicting and moderating views.** Textbook writers still group by theme because it looks tidy. Some later classroom studies find a benefit or a null for semantic sets, especially when learners already know some of the set, when pictures reduce confusion, or when the “cluster” is really a story. Hashemi and Gowdasiaei (2005) reported an advantage for semantic sets at a higher proficiency. Erten and Tekin (2008) supported interference for young learners. Ishii (2015) argued that visually similar items, not just semantic category, can be the problem. Nation’s practical advice is closer to Tinkham than to the textbook: do not present a new colour set or a new animal set as the first meeting of those words.

**How to use it.** “Food unit: apple, pear, peach, plum, grape” is the interference design. A page of words that showed up in one conversation is closer to a thematic set. This is a reason some *course* lists fail, not a reason all lists fail.

**Strength.** Medium-high for the original experiments; mixed in later classroom work. Do not present it as settled law. It is still one of the better explanations for why a themed beginner pack feels busy and then collapses.

**Citations.**

## 7. Text coverage (95–98%)

**Finding.** Hu and Nation (2000) rewrote a fiction text so that unknown-word density was 0%, 5%, 10%, or 20% (coverage 100%, 95%, 90%, 80%). Adequate unassisted comprehension, on their measures, required 98% coverage for most readers. Almost no one read adequately at 80% or 90%. Laufer’s earlier classroom work had pointed to about 95% as a floor for “reasonable” comprehension. Laufer and Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) split the threshold: a *minimal* threshold around 95% coverage, about 4,000–5,000 word families including proper nouns; an *optimal* threshold around 98%, about 8,000 families. Nation (2006) then asked how large a vocabulary those coverage figures imply. For 98% of novels and newspapers he estimated 8,000–9,000 word families; for 98% of spoken English, 6,000–7,000. About 4,000 families plus proper nouns reached 95% of the novels. Kremmel, Indrarathne, Kormos, and Suzuki (2023) replicated Hu and Nation’s density design. The coverage–comprehension link held; some details of the original 98% cutoff are less absolute than textbook citations suggest.

**How to use it.** A frequency list is a map of English, not a substitute for the text. If you only know list items, and the page in front of you is 10% unknown, you are in Hu and Nation’s 90% condition. The article should not say “therefore never study high-frequency words.” It should say that words have to be known *in running language*, and that a decontextualized list does not create that coverage by itself.

**Strength.** Strong for the coverage–comprehension relationship. The exact 95 versus 98 cut is a range, not a law. The jump from “you need 98% coverage” to “generic word lists fail” is an argument, not a finding. Keep that jump visible.

## 8. Interest / self-selection

**Finding.** There is no single gold-standard experiment titled “learner-chosen word list versus assigned frequency list.” The usable evidence is adjacent. Elley (1989) had 7- and 8-year-olds listen to stories read aloud three times, with teachers explaining some words. Vocabulary gains were large, and the words children rated as more interesting were learned better. Joe (1998) found that generative tasks on a text (retelling, using the word) produced more incidental vocabulary learning than less generative exposure. Laufer and Hulstijn (2001) proposed involvement load: tasks high in need, search, and evaluation produce more incidental learning than low-involvement tasks. Hidi (2001) reviews interest as a motivator that increases attention and persistence; that is a general learning claim, not a vocabulary RCT.

**How to use it.** A word you asked for after a conversation has need. A word from unit 12 of a book you are not reading has little. This supports “collect what you meet” more than it condemns frequency lists. Assigned high-frequency words can still be worth learning; they just need a reason and a later retrieval.

**Strength.** Weakest of the nine as a direct causal claim. Elley is about listening to stories, not about self-composed word lists. Involvement load has mixed later tests. Do not write “science shows self-chosen words are always remembered better.” Write that interest and a reason to need the word are associated with more attention and more generation, which are known to help.

**Citations.**

## 9. Word lists versus reading / context

**Finding.** Direct comparisons do *not* say “lists lose, reading wins” on every measure.

Prince (1996) compared L1-translation learning with learning from L2 sentence context. Translation was more efficient for remembering form–meaning. Weaker learners especially failed to use contextual clues. Context helped some learners use the word later, but it was not a free lunch.

Laufer (2003) asked whether learners “really learn most vocabulary by reading.” In her comparisons, word-focused tasks beat reading-only for the target items. She argues that unassisted reading does not deliver enough encounters or enough attention for most new words.

Webb (2007) showed that more repetitions in reading improve multiple aspects of word knowledge, but gains per encounter are small. Waring and Takaki (2003) found that after reading a graded reader, even words met 15 or more times were incompletely known a few months later, especially on productive tests. Pigada and Schmitt (2006) followed one learner through a month of extensive reading: spelling and meaning improved, but far from completely, and not equally for every word.

Nation’s own position, including in Webb and Nation (2017), is the one the article should steal: deliberate learning (word cards, lists with retrieval) is a fast way to attach a form to a meaning; incidental learning from input is slow but builds collocation, register, and use. The failure is using *only* a generic list, or expecting reading alone to replace any deliberate work.

**How to use it.** “Why word lists fail” is true of *generic, unread, untested* lists. It is false of a short, retrieved, contextualized list of words you met. Folse (2004) is useful as a brake: the myth that “lists are bad” is itself overstated.

**Strength.** Strong if the article keeps the split between form–meaning (lists can win) and use / durable knowledge (lists alone lose). Weak if the article claims experiments show list learning is useless.

## Contested or weak (quick flags)

| Claim | Status | Article advice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exact “forget 70% in a day” | Popularization of savings, not free recall of words | Use the shape, not the meme number |
| Depth of processing | Strong as encoding quality; not an L2 list experiment | Cite Craik and Tulving, then step to Joe / involvement load |
| Must restudy in the same room | Godden and Baddeley overread; Smith and Vela: small effect | Mention context as cues in the *language*, not the furniture |
| Semantic sets always hurt | Tinkham / Finkbeiner yes; later classroom mixed | “Often interferes on first meeting,” plus thematic exception |
| 98% coverage is a hard law | Range 95–98; 2023 replication softens the cutoff | Call it a band |
| Self-chosen words always stick | Indirect (interest, involvement) | Soft claim only |
| Lists are useless | Contradicted by Prince, Nation, Folse | Attack generic unread lists, not retrieval cards |
