Lately, I have been opening ChatGPT every evening for fifteen minutes of French conversation. For the first few weeks, having a language partner available at any time felt great. There was no appointment to make, and it did not mind my accent. After a while, though, I noticed a problem: when I closed the window, almost nothing remained. Speaking felt a little smoother in the moment, but the verb form that stopped me two days ago stopped me again today. Yesterday's wrong preposition returned unchanged.

A common way to use ChatGPT as a free language partner is to give it a short instruction such as, “Talk to me in French and correct my mistakes.” After the conversation, you might copy a few new words into Notion or Anki. That depends heavily on how much energy you have that day. It also leaves several practical questions unanswered: where exactly should each item go, what happens to the card after you organise it, and where will the context come from when it is time to speak again?

I changed the routine so that speaking practice would leave a record. ChatGPT still holds the conversation, but afterwards it also organises the vocabulary, mistakes, and more natural alternatives that actually appeared in that session. Those results go into Notion, which keeps the topic, status, and review progress. A disposable chat becomes something I can return to.

## Give the language partner a specific job

I ask ChatGPT to do more than keep the conversation moving. It controls the level, corrects me during the session, collects useful vocabulary, and, most importantly, shows my sentence beside a more natural version.

Here is the opening instruction I use:

**You are now my French language partner. Keep the vocabulary and grammar between A2 and B1. Choose an everyday topic and ask only one question at a time. After I answer in French, you must do three things: 1. identify the grammar or word-choice mistakes in my answer and correct them clearly; 2. list two or three new words from this exchange; 3. rewrite what I just said as more natural, idiomatic everyday French. Only after completing those three steps should you ask the next question.**

Without an explicit requirement to show mistakes and rewrites, the model often keeps the conversation pleasant and skips errors that do not block understanding. That is why my instruction says that the comparison is mandatory.

## Speaking is retrieval practice

Input is only half of the routine; the output needs somewhere to go. Research by Roediger and Karpicke on test-enhanced learning found that active recall supports long-term retention better than simply rereading material. Their later paper in *Science* examined the importance of retrieval during learning.

Trying to say what you mean in another language is retrieval practice. You have to search for a particular word and assemble a sentence from the grammar you know.

Speaking alone is not enough, however. The useful parts of the conversation still need a fixed place to land.

## Catch each conversation on a board

I created a conversation database and a French speaking board in Notion. General conversation material goes into the database first. When I am ready to practise, I choose one clear topic and create a “To organise” card on the board. After the session, ChatGPT uses that real exchange to organise the most useful vocabulary, mistakes, and natural alternatives on the same card, along with the practice date and status.

The board has four columns:

- To organise
- Practised once
- Review needed
- Organised

Cards do not move automatically through a simple pipeline. After the first conversation, a “To organise” card moves to “Practised once”. If I later notice that I still do not know the material, I drag it to “Review needed”. Only after another real review does it move to “Organised”.

I also limit each card to one speaking goal and usually keep only three to five groups of core sentences. If one conversation covers several topics, or clearly contains more than five groups worth keeping, I split it into several cards rather than storing the whole transcript on one page. I do not collect vocabulary for its own sake. I keep only words and phrases that belong to that card and that I might genuinely say again.

This board also creates distributed practice. A 2006 review by Cepeda and colleagues found that spreading study across different days generally works better than concentrating it into one session.

## Keep only what really happened

Everything worth reviewing comes from the actual practice: what I really said and the corrections, explanations, and natural rewrites ChatGPT gave during that session.

A spoken sentence may be incomplete because of a pause, pronunciation, or speech recognition. In that case, I can keep the complete correction that ChatGPT explicitly gave at the time. I do not invent missing material later just to make the notes look complete.

This connects with the argument in [Why Word Lists Fail](/blog/why-word-lists-fail). A generic list made by somebody else has no personal context, so its words are hard to connect with your own speech. The word you struggled to produce in a real conversation carries the situation, the frustration, and the thing you wanted to say. It is usually easier to retrieve when you need it again.

## A short daily routine

My speaking practice now comes down to a short list:

- Open ChatGPT each day and have a brief conversation about one everyday topic.
- Start with explicit rules: show the mistakes, collect useful vocabulary, and provide more natural rewrites.
- After the conversation, save the three to five most useful sentence groups in Notion and move the card to “Practised once”.
- Browse the board occasionally. When a conversation starts to fade, drag it to “Review needed”.
- Set aside a review day. Open that column, revisit the sentences that caused trouble and the natural rewrites, then move the card to “Organised” only after doing the review.

This routine is deliberately small. I do not spend hours memorising difficult words I will never use, and I do not begin each day with a blank document. I speak, keep the useful parts, and use the board to remind myself when it is time to return. Language builds through concrete exchanges and regular review.
