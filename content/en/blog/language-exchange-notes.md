## Notes that never get opened again

A language learner studying French and Japanese once asked a language exchange community how to take notes during a language exchange. Their own answer: they almost never took notes during sessions, and when they did write something down, they almost never looked at it again. They had tried making Anki cards for every useful expression and found the whole routine too heavy. They wrote things down and never reopened them, so one conversation never became material for the next one.

## Why a language exchange needs notes more than a class does

Spoken lines are gone when the call ends. A textbook page is still there. A natural phrasing, a small correction, or slang your partner dropped casually: you understood it in the moment. By tomorrow, all that is left is "I think we said something about that."

Without review, memory fades quickly, and the steepest drop happens in the first hours. Replications of the classic forgetting curve put retention at roughly 58 percent after 20 minutes, 44 percent after one hour, and 34 percent after one day ([Murre & Dros, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4492928/)).

Spoken language is largely made of ready-made phrases rather than single words assembled by grammar rules ([Ellis, Simpson-Vlach & Maynard, 2008](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/nickellis-new/wp-content/uploads/sites/1284/2021/07/TQ.pdf)). The useful things to write down are natural phrasings and corrections you can reuse as they are, not a vocabulary list. That is also why [generic word lists fail](/blog/why-word-lists-fail): words you never used in a conversation have nothing to attach to.

## Decide what to write down

Keep the list small. In one exchange, write down:

- words, phrases, and natural phrasings you want to say next time
- the corrections your partner gave you
- topics you want to talk about or practice next time

Do not transcribe the whole conversation. One learner limits themselves to a 5×7 card and stops when it is full. A card that gets reopened beats a full notebook that does not.

## During the session: write without interrupting

What some learners do:

- Drop an unknown word into the chat box so both of you can see it, then keep talking instead of stopping to organize
- Keep one small card or a few scratch lines at hand; jot the words, and leave the organizing for later

Some learners write a short passage of about five minutes before the session to warm up pronunciation and questions. That is preparation, not note-taking.

You do not have to write by hand during the call. Writing by hand is linked to broader brain activity and forces you to filter while you write ([Van der Meer & van der Weel, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945/full)), and some learners swear by paper. The point is to catch things during the session and sort them out afterward; the tool matters less.

## The fifteen minutes after: decide which notes to keep

Right after the session, copy what you caught from the chat box or the card into one fixed notebook. For each entry, add one line in your own words: who said it, in what situation, and how you will use it next time. Some learners cross the card out and throw it away once it has been transcribed.

Do not turn every phrase into flashcards on the spot. If you are willing to review, you can build cards later; if cards are not for you, handwritten re-reading plus forced reuse at the next exchange works too. The research behind spaced review says the timing matters more than the tool ([Noor et al., 2021](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1313692.pdf)). One learner found that easy notes were forgotten, and the ones that took effort were the ones they still knew.

## What belongs to your partner

The original question also asked whether to share notes with the partner, and the replies mostly dodged it:

- your whole private notebook is not something to dump on the other person
- words dropped into the chat box are temporary and shared, not a long-term notebook
- what is worth sending is the filtered part: corrections, more natural phrasings, and suggestions for next time

## A minimal system without Anki

If the full card routine still feels like too much, this is the smallest working version:

- Before the exchange, prepare one phrasing you want to practice
- During the exchange, catch a limited number of words
- After the exchange, transcribe them and mark the ones for next time
- At the next exchange, use last time's three items first
- If you are willing, add what you recorded for yourself to your review pile

Catching new words in the session and reviewing them afterward is exactly what language exchange guides recommend ([Tandem](https://tandem.net/blog/reinforce-learning-language-exchange)). Anki is optional; random word lists are what make the routine heavy. The learner who kept cards only for things they actually wanted to learn found the cards stopped being boring.
