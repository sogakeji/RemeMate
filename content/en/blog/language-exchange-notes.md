## The notes everyone writes and everyone loses

A language learner studying French and Japanese once asked a language exchange community a practical question: how do you take notes during a language exchange? Their own answer was honest. During sessions they almost never took notes. When they did write something down, they almost never looked at it again. They had tried making Anki cards for every useful expression and found the whole routine too heavy.

The problem was never taking notes or not taking notes. It was writing things down and never reopening them, so one conversation never became material for the next one.

## Why a language exchange needs notes more than a class does

Conversation disappears faster than a textbook page. The natural phrasing, the small correction, the slang your partner dropped casually — you understood it in the moment, and by tomorrow all that is left is "I think we said something about that."

Without review, memory fades quickly, and the steepest drop happens in the first hours. Replications of the classic forgetting curve put retention at roughly 58 percent after 20 minutes, 44 percent after one hour, and 34 percent after one day ([Murre & Dros, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4492928/)). Writing a sentence and never seeing it again is close to never writing it.

Notes also matter for a second reason. Spoken language is largely made of ready-made phrases rather than single words assembled by grammar rules ([Ellis, Simpson-Vlach & Maynard, 2008](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/nickellis-new/wp-content/uploads/sites/1284/2021/07/TQ.pdf)). The most valuable things to catch are therefore natural phrasings and corrections — units you can reuse whole — instead of a vocabulary list. That is also why [generic word lists fail](/blog/why-word-lists-fail): words without a meeting have nothing to attach to.

## Decide what to take before you decide where to write it

Keep the intake deliberately small. In one exchange, catch three kinds of things:

- words, phrases, and natural phrasings you want to say next time
- the corrections your partner gave you
- topics you want to talk about or practice next time

Do not transcribe the whole conversation. One learner limits themselves to a 5×7 card and stops when it is full. The limit is the point: a notebook that is full but never opened is worse than a card that is full and gets reopened.

## During the session: write without interrupting

Habits from learners who actually do this:

- Drop an unknown word into the chat box so both of you can see it, then keep talking instead of stopping to organize
- Keep one small card or a few scratch lines at hand; write words, not a lesson
- Before the session, some learners write a short passage of about five minutes to warm up pronunciation and questions — that is preparation, not note-taking

Writing by hand is linked to broader brain activity and forces you to filter while you write ([Van der Meer & van der Weel, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1219945/full)), and some learners swear by paper. But handwriting in the moment is not the only legitimate method. The rule that matters is: catch it while it is live, process it afterward.

## The fifteen minutes after: decide whether a note survives

Right after the session, copy what you caught from the chat box or the card into one fixed notebook. For each entry, add one line in your own words: who said it, in what situation, and how you will use it next time. Some learners cross the card out and throw it away once it has been transcribed.

Do not turn every phrase into flashcards on the spot. If you are willing to review, you can build cards later; if cards are not for you, handwritten re-reading plus forced reuse at the next exchange works too. The research behind spaced review says the timing matters more than the tool ([Noor et al., 2021](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1313692.pdf)). One learner noticed the same pattern the poster later described: easy notes slip away, and the ones that cost effort are the ones that stay.

## What belongs to your partner

The original question also asked whether to share notes with the partner, and the replies mostly dodged it. The honest answer has three parts:

- your whole private notebook is not something to dump on the other person
- words dropped into the chat box are temporary and shared, not a long-term notebook
- what is worth sending is the filtered part: corrections, more natural phrasings, and suggestions for next time

That is the boundary the product we built around language partners follows. What you record for yourself stays private by default. What you record for your partner is filtered before it becomes a feedback pack. Your private partner notes are never sent to an AI summary, and a partner you connect with cannot see your history or your word library.

## A minimal system without Anki

If the full card routine still feels like too much, this is the smallest working version:

- Before the exchange, prepare one phrasing you want to practice
- During the exchange, catch a limited number of words
- After the exchange, transcribe them and mark the ones for next time
- At the next exchange, use last time's three items first
- If you are willing, fold what you recorded for yourself into review

Catching new words in the session and reviewing them afterward is exactly what language exchange guides recommend ([Tandem](https://tandem.net/blog/reinforce-learning-language-exchange)). Anki is not the villain; random word lists are the burden. The learner who kept cards only for things they actually wanted to learn found the cards stopped being boring — and that is the real test of any note system.

## Make the exchange a learning asset

The value of a language exchange is not that you talked. It is that you walk away with material you can review and produce again. Around one language partner, you can keep what you want to remember for yourself, send your partner only the filtered feedback, and let them decide whether to take it into their own words. No real-time co-editing, no automatic harvesting of the whole conversation, no speaking scores. Just notes that survive the next twenty-four hours.
