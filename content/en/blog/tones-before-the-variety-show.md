She calls herself smolbiskit, and also Emi. Her About text is a greeting, not a CV: "Hey guys. I do language related videos." YouTube lists the channel under Entertainment, not Education. Public pages do not name a second job. What is on the record is the claim in her titles: she learned fluent Mandarin Chinese, self-taught.

A search-indexed line from the video puts tones first. In Mandarin, pitch contour changes the meaning of a syllable: rising, falling, or falling then rising. She began with tones, which distinguish otherwise identical syllables. Beyond a TikTok she links, a short about Chinese tones that she made or saved, the videos do not describe her tone drills.

The first title already claims fluency. A second video, posted a few weeks later, is titled *How I Learned Fluent Mandarin Chinese | Through TV Shows*, which specifies the picture the first one left out: TV shows. In the description she points to a playlist: WayV's *Dream Plan*. The show is talk, games, and leftover chatter between tasks, informal speech rather than scripted lessons. With tones in place, that chatter is usable input, not only background noise.

She also names other sources: a YouTuber, Kelly Yang; a Chinative playlist; two podcasts, *Learning Chinese Through Stories* and one whose Chinese title is 旅歐三小事. No teacher is assigning the next page; these are the things she actually pressed play on. She is putting Mandarin in her ears on purpose, using shows and podcasts made to be watched or listened to, not only to be studied.

There is a FluentU link in the description. She does not say she learned with it. The link reads as an ad; it is not evidence of her method. The method on the record is the one in her titles and in the names of the shows.

In her usage, "self-taught" does not mean she never heard another person. It means she does not credit an institution for her Mandarin. The voices she credits are the public ones already named.

The two videos sit close together in late 2025. Both return to the same claim, how she learned fluent Mandarin, first as self-taught, then through television. She does not narrate a stay in a Chinese-speaking city; the practice she describes is screen-based. The materials are a tone short, long variety episodes, and podcasts in headphones. Repetition, as described, is pressing play again when an episode, a tone short, or a story-podcast installment ends.
