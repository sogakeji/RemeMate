## The list everyone owns and no one finishes

Almost every language learner has one: a downloaded "1000 most common words" file, unit 12 of a textbook, a deck built from someone else's list. The first twenty entries feel like progress. Somewhere around the middle the list starts to blur, and by the end it becomes a thing to feel guilty about rather than a thing to use.

The list itself is not the enemy. Plenty of words can be learned from a list, and the research is honest about that. What fails is a particular habit: a generic list, studied once, never reviewed, with no connection to anything you read or hear. That habit fails almost by design, and the reasons are well documented.

## A list you never reopen behaves like a memory that was never there

The forgetting curve is the oldest finding in memory research. Ebbinghaus measured how much faster relearning was after different delays, and a modern replication matched the classic curve ([Murre & Dros, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4492928/)): savings fell to about 58 percent after 20 minutes, 44 percent after one hour, and 34 percent after one day, with the steepest drop in the first hours. Coming back to relearn is much cheaper than starting over — but only if you come back.

A word list that is studied once and never reopened follows exactly this curve. The honest version of the claim: those numbers describe savings, not "you forget 70 percent of vocabulary in a day". What they mean for a list is simple — without a revisit, the trace fades to almost nothing and the words become strangers again.

## Knowing a list is not the same as being able to read

A frequency list is a map of a language, not the language. Comprehension research is blunt about this. Hu and Nation (2000) rewrote fiction with 0, 5, 10, or 20 percent unknown words; most readers needed around 98 percent coverage of running words for adequate unassisted comprehension, and almost nobody managed at 80 or 90 percent ([Hu & Nation, 2000](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/43)). Later work splits the threshold: around 95 percent coverage as a minimum, around 98 percent as the comfortable zone ([Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski, 2010](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/206)). Nation (2006) estimates that 98 percent of novels and newspapers needs roughly 8,000 to 9,000 word families ([Nation, 2006](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf)).

So a list of "the 2,000 most common words" is real and worth learning, but it does not by itself put those words into the texts you actually meet. If a page in front of you is ten percent unknown, you are sitting in the 90 percent coverage condition — the one where comprehension collapses.

## Retrieval, not rereading, is what sticks

There is a reliable finding under the name test-enhanced learning: trying to recall something makes it stick better than studying it again, even when the extra study feels more productive in the moment ([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2006/2006_Roediger_Karpicke_PsychSci.pdf)). A follow-up showed that repeated retrieval was the critical part of learning that lasts ([Karpicke & Roediger, 2008](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2008/2008_Karpicke_Roediger_Science.pdf)).

Reading a list top to bottom is rereading. Highlighting it is rereading. The list only starts working when it stops being a list and starts being a prompt: cover the word, try to say it, check. This is also why the practical advice in our article on [language exchange notes](/blog/language-exchange-notes) is to catch a few words and actually reuse them next time — retrieval, not repetition.

## Spacing beats cramming

Cramming a 500-word list over one weekend is massed practice, and massed practice loses. A meta-analysis of 317 experiments found distributed practice consistently beat massing, and the useful gap grows with how long you want to remember ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://augmentingcognition.com/assets/Cepeda2006.pdf)). Independent reviews rate both practice testing and distributed practice as high-utility techniques ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26173288/)).

Meeting fifteen of those words again next week, and fifteen more the month after, beats one long weekend with all five hundred. A printed chapter list rarely does that on its own; a review system does.

## The theme pack can work against you

There is a specific way some lists fail that learners usually do not expect: studying a whole semantic set at once can interfere with learning it. New words presented as a category — all fruits, all furniture, all colours — took more trials to learn than unrelated or story-linked sets ([Tinkham, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0346-251X(93)90027-E); [1997](https://doi.org/10.1191/026765897672376469)), and category presentation slowed both encoding and later translation of new labels ([Finkbeiner & Nicol, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0142716403000195)). Later classroom studies are more mixed, so this is not settled law. But the practical advice from the same line of research is: do not meet a brand-new colour set or animal set as your first meeting of those words.

A page of words that showed up in one real conversation is a different shape. That is closer to a thematic set — the kind that helps — which is one more reason conversation-caught words behave better than unit 12.

## The honest counterpoint: lists can teach form and meaning fast

None of this means lists are useless. Compared with learning from context alone, translation-list study is often faster for remembering form and meaning ([Prince, 1996](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1996.tb05468.x)), and word-focused tasks beat reading-only for the target words ([Laufer, 2003](https://doi.org/10.3138/cmlr.59.4.567)). Weaker learners in particular struggle to pick up words from context without help.

So the failure mode is specific: a generic list — unreviewed, never retrieved, studied as one whole theme pack, with no meeting in real language. Flashcards built from words you actually need, reviewed on a spaced schedule, with a context line attached, are a different animal. Anki was never the villain; the random list is.

## What works instead

The research points to a short list of habits that survive contact with reality:

- meet the word in something you actually read or hear
- retrieve it later, from memory, on a spaced schedule
- keep a short context or example with the word
- do not study a whole semantic set in one sitting
- prefer words you have a reason to need

That is the shape of the system in our article on [language exchange notes](/blog/language-exchange-notes): catch a few words during an exchange, tidy them into fixed notes, mark the ones for next time, and reuse them. A word list becomes a learning asset only when it stops being a list — and starts being material you meet again, retrieve, and use.
