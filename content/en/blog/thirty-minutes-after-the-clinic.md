Delancey introduces herself the way a neighbor might, not the way a school would. Hello in English, then 안녕하세요, then 你好. Her name is Delancey. She makes slice-of-life videos about a language journey in Korean and Mandarin, and about building a cozy home with her Chinese boyfriend. The tags around the channel are almost domestic: cozy content, dietitian life, registered dietitian, Korean culture, Chinese culture, Taiwanese culture. She is not selling a method so much as filming a week.

The week in question has a job in it. The title says so twice, once in English and once in the stubborn fact of a 9-to-5: *How I Study Korean (with a full time job)*. In the description she writes it again. This week she will try to study Korean at least thirty minutes a day during a busy work week. She asks, with the honesty of someone who already knows the answer might be no, whether she will meet all her goals.

Thirty minutes is not a legend. It is a kitchen timer. A registered dietitian’s day is already full of other people’s meals, charts, the kind of attention that does not leave leftover grandeur for a second career in linguistics. Korean has to fit in the same life that already contains a clinic, a home, a partner, and another language she is also carrying — Mandarin — without turning either one into a performance of purity. She is learning both. The video on the table is about Korean, because one week can only hold one experiment cleanly.

She writes down the tools as if she is packing a bag. Anki, the flashcard app. A Pinterest board of Korean vocabulary. A podcast, DiDi’s Korean Culture Podcast. A Talk To Me in Korean vlog playlist. The list is ordinary on purpose. None of these objects require her to become a different person at 6 a.m. They require her to open something she can close again when the next patient, or the next pot, arrives.

The chapters of the vlog are the true schedule. Preview and intro. Then goals. Then study tools. Then the challenge begins. Then tutor prep. Then a tutor session and a reflection. Then studying on the go. Then vocab time. Then her favorite TTMIK playlist. Then grammar lessons. Then speaking practice, and the outro. Read in order, the week has a weather. She does not only drill cards. She prepares for a person. She meets the person. She thinks about the meeting afterward. She studies while moving. She returns to words. She lets a playlist teach. She sits with grammar. She tries to speak.

The tutor is real enough to have a chapter, and real enough that the video is tagged with an italki teacher hashtag. The description does not lock the platform name into a sentence, and so the story should not either. What can be said is simpler. There is a lesson with a teacher. There is homework in the shape of prep. There is a reflection after. For a person whose paid work is already talking and listening in another register — food, bodies, plans — the Korean lesson is a second kind of appointment. It has to be prepared for in the cracks, and it has to be recovered from in the cracks.

On-the-go is the chapter that keeps the story from becoming a desk myth. She does not claim a library carrel. She claims motion. A commute, a walk, the leftover minutes between one room and another. Thirty minutes a day, if it is going to survive a 9-to-5, will not always look like a seated scholar. Sometimes it will look like a phone and a pair of headphones and the refusal to let the interval be only tiredness.

Vocabulary time is its own stamp on the timeline, separate from the tutor and separate from the grammar. She has a Pinterest board for this. The image is almost funny in its softness: a dietitian, a cozy-home channel, a pinboard of words. It is also exact. She is collecting language the way she might collect recipes, visually, in a place she already knows how to open at night.

Talk To Me in Korean is not a personality cult in her telling. It is a playlist she likes, given a chapter, used in a week when motivation has to be cheaper than inspiration. Grammar gets its own later chapter, which means she did not pretend the playlist replaced it. Speaking gets the last study stamp before the goodbye. The order is not a manifesto. It is what she actually did, or tried to do, while employed.

Hesitation is written into the premise. *Will I meet all my goals?* is not a rhetorical sparkle. A challenge video that asks the question in the description has already admitted that a work week can win. The thirty minutes are a dare she might fail in public. That is why the piece belongs in a series about ordinary learners. A person with a license and a clinic and a home does not owe the internet a perfect streak. She owes it, if she owes it anything, a week she was willing to film.

There is no verified scene of the boyfriend speaking Mandarin at the table, and so that scene will not be written. What exists is the About line: she is building a cozy home with him, and Mandarin is part of the same channel as Korean. Two languages, one household, one job. The Korean week is a slice, not the whole fruit.

The aftertaste is the timer. Not a transformation. A dietitian closing a door, opening Anki or a pin or a playlist, giving Korean half an hour she could have given to the couch, then going back to the life that pays the rent. The next day she has to do it again. That repetition is the method, and she said so by making the method a challenge.
